Recipe cards — six flavors

what goes on the face is the whole decision · real recipes, real runs

A card only beats a row when it has a face — something identifying that a line of text can't carry. Seven text boxes in a grid are strictly worse than seven rows: more space, less scanning, no vertical comparison. So each flavor below is really an answer to one question: what is the face made of?

Five answers here — the recipe itself, its last output, its rhythm, its parts, and an identity you assign — plus one that mixes sizes instead of making them uniform. The last frame stress-tests the two that scale.

1 · The index card — face = the recipe, written like a recipe

Leans all the way into the name. Serif title, a rule, then the four things a recipe card always tells you: what it makes, how long it takes, what's in it, and how often you make it. This is the anatomy grammar from the first study, wearing the clothes the metaphor implies.

Fixes: the most self-explanatory card of the six — a new user learns what a recipe is by reading one. "Makes / uses / when" is a better vocabulary than "outputs / integrations / schedule", and the ~time and $/mo footers answer questions the list never has room for.

Costs: it breaks two rules from the settled spec on purpose — uppercase micro-labels and a second type family in the list. It also has no face at all in the visual sense: seven of these in a grid is still seven boxes of text, distinguishable only by reading. Charming up close, mush at a glance.

2 · The poster — face = the last thing it said, as a pull quote

Two-up, so the face gets room to be actual content. A chart-publishing recipe shows its chart; a prose recipe shows a real pull quote in the reader serif. The recipe name demotes to the caption underneath, the way a photo credit sits under a photo.

3 · The rhythm card — face = when it fires and how it's been going

The face is a 24-hour line with the fire time marked, and a week of outcome bars beneath it. Cards become sortable by cadence visually — the 6 AM cluster reads differently from the manual one without reading a word.

Fixes: the only face that says something a row genuinely can't — the shape of your day. Scheduling collisions become visible (three recipes clustered at 6–8 AM), and a schedule is the one property you actually tune repeatedly.

Costs: it's a dashboard face, not a content face — it tells you about the machinery rather than the output, which is the ops-tool trap from two studies ago. Also weakest exactly where you need it most: the manual recipe's clock is empty.

4 · The kit card — face = the parts, compact, four-up

The anatomy line as a card. Small, dense, scannable, and the only flavor that keeps working at scale. Broken parts turn red in place, so the grid is also a wiring diagnostic.

5 · The cover — face = an identity you assign

Every recipe gets an emoji and a tint, picked when you create it or suggested by Pi. It's the cheapest possible face and the most effective for recognition — you stop reading names and start recognising shapes, the way you find an app by its icon rather than its label.

6 · Hero and shelves — stop making every card the same size

Uniform grids say every recipe matters equally, which is never true on any given morning. Here the one that needs you gets a wide card that states the problem and offers the fix, and the rest sit in shelves grouped by cadence — the grouping you actually think in.

Which face earns the space

FlavorFace isBest atBreaks when
1 · Index cardthe recipe, explainedteaching a new user what a recipe isyou have more than a few — all boxes look alike
2 · Posterthe last outputmaking the library worth looking ata recipe has never run; only 4 fit on screen
3 · Rhythmthe schedule + weekspotting collisions and tuning cadencethe recipe is manual; it's machinery, not content
4 · Kitthe partsdiagnosing wiring at any scaleit's just a row in a box — loses the column scan
5 · Coveran assigned identityrecognition; reusable everywhere elsethe user won't pick one; says nothing on its own
6 · Hero + shelvesmixed by importancehaving a point of view about todaynothing is broken and the top zone vanishes

My pick is 5 + 2 together, and it's really one idea: give each recipe an emoji identity (5) so it's recognisable everywhere in the app, and let the card face show its latest output (2) so the library is worth opening. That's a poster card with a cover mark — the identity does the finding, the output does the convincing.

But the honest answer to "should this be cards at all": for seven recipes, yes, and the reason is creation as much as browsing — a grid has a natural empty slot that says ＋ New recipe, which a list never does as gracefully. At forty, the grid becomes a launcher and you'll want the anatomy table back. So build the card grid and keep a list toggle; don't pick one forever.
