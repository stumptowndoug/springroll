Inspector, and five other ways out

exploratory · the first three are new shapes, not new arrangements

Four studies of rearranging rows haven't landed, and that's worth taking seriously as evidence. Every one of them answered how should the list look. None of them answered what am I here to do — and a page without a job can be beautifully arranged and still feel like nothing.

Four different jobs, and they want opposite layouts:
  • Triage — "is anything broken?" wants a page that is empty on a good day.
  • Read — "what did I learn?" wants prose, one column, no chrome.
  • Monitor — "is the machine healthy?" wants density, history, cost.
  • Explore — "what happened with X over time?" wants search and pivoting, not a list at all.
Everything so far has quietly tried to serve all four at once, which is why each version feels like it's missing something — it is. Below, each surface commits to one.

1 · The inspector — what you asked for, three ways

Prior art: Linear (list + detail panel, j/k to move) · Superhuman split view · Finder's inspector · Notion peek

List gets narrow and quiet — one line per item. The panel carries everything else. The key decision isn't whether to have a panel, it's what the panel is: a fixed detail pane, an overlay you dismiss, or a stack you drill into. All three below, same data.

Fixes: the list finally gets to be minimal, because it no longer has to carry everything — one line, one dot, one time. And the stack version is the interesting one for your actual problem: one panel serves both Reports and Recipes, so the two pages stop needing different detail treatments.

Costs: panels want a wide window, and they compete with the berth for the right edge and the bottom. They also punish mobile, where a panel becomes a full-screen push — at which point you've built navigation and called it a panel. The peek variant is the one that degrades most gracefully.

2 · Zoom, not drill — the same list at three resolutions

Prior art: Gmail's density setting · map zoom levels · Finder icon-size slider · Bear's line-height modes

A genuinely different answer to "minimal, then expand": don't add a second surface — change the resolution of the one you have. One control in the header. Level 1 is a manifest you scan in two seconds; level 3 renders the reports themselves, and the page becomes today's reading. No panel, no navigation, no detail page.

3 · The quiet page — on a good day this is nearly empty

Prior art: GitHub's "All checks have passed ▾" · Sentry's resolved collapse · Superhuman's Inbox Zero · Healthchecks' green wall

Costs: it optimizes hard for triage and treats reading as a second click, which is backwards if the reports are the product. Pairs naturally with zoom (2) — the quiet page is level 0.

4 · The week — past and future on one grid

Prior art: Google Calendar · Fantastical · Cron · GitHub's contribution graph

A calendar is the only widely-understood surface that shows what happened and what will happen in the same view — which is exactly the Reports/Recipes duality, solved by a metaphor everyone already knows. Solid blocks are reports that exist and can be read; dashed ones are scheduled and can be skipped, rescheduled or run early. Drag one to change its schedule.

Fixes: the schedule stops being a text string you edit in a form and becomes a thing you can see and move. Failure patterns become spatial — the AssessorSearch row going red across Saturday and Sunday tells a story a list of rows never will. And a Recipe is now legible as a repeating row, which is what it actually is.

Costs: daily recipes make every column identical, so the grid is highly redundant — seven copies of the same pattern to show two failures. It also collapses badly for anything running hourly or every 15 minutes, and manual recipes have nowhere to live but an "any" row.

5 · The logbook — no rows at all; the workspace is one continuous document

Prior art: Obsidian daily notes · a lab notebook · an engineering logbook · Day One journals

The most complete break from the list. There are no rows, no cards, no panels — just a running document with a rule between days, and each report sitting in it as an entry. You don't navigate this page, you read backwards through it. Everything else in the app becomes a way to filter which entries appear.

Fixes: it is the only surface here that feels like it belongs to a product about reading, and it fits the editorial direction the rest of the app has been moving toward. It also makes the whole tasks/runs argument moot — recipes become a filter over the log, not a page.

Costs: hopeless for management. You cannot see what you own, what it costs, or what's wired up, and finding a specific old report means search or infinite scroll. This is a home page that needs a real second surface behind it — probably the anatomy table from study one.

6 · Ask first — the list is the fallback, not the surface

Prior art: Raycast · Superhuman command palette · Perplexity's home · the fact that you already put Pi on every page

The most heretical option: don't lead with a list at all. You already have a chat band on every page and a model that can read every report. So the home page is a question, with suggestions derived from what actually happened today — and the list of reports is a thin strip underneath for when you'd rather browse.

If I had to bet on what you're circling: 3 + 5. The quiet page as the front door (triage in three seconds, empty on a good day) opening into the logbook (the reading, backwards through time). That pair has an actual opinion — most days there is nothing to manage, so the app should show you what it found and get out of the way — and it's the only combination here where the machinery is genuinely absent until something breaks.

2 is the safe answer and 1 is the conventional one. 4 and 6 are the two I'd prototype even if you don't ship them, because both would teach you something about the product that no amount of row design will.
