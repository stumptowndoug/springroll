Integrations, five ways.

Today the tab is a two-column grid of equal-height cards where every provider — a live Gmail account, a one-click connector, an unbuilt “coming soon” tile — gets the same 160px of real estate. The result is a page that is mostly whitespace, says “Coming soon” twice per card, and makes a connection you actually depend on look identical to one that does not exist yet.

Each direction below drops the card grid and picks a different organizing idea. The split that keeps re-appearing — your live connections vs. the catalog — is treated as a question, not a given: options 1, 2 and 4 dissolve it entirely, 3 and 5 keep it but make it earn its place through state rather than through connector type.

01One list

The idea

No sections at all. Every provider is one row in a single ranked list — connected first, then connectable, then unbuilt. Type of connector never partitions the page; state orders it, and filter chips replace the headings.

Trade-off

Dense and honest — 14 providers fit where 4 cards used to. Loses the browsable, logo-forward feel of a catalog, so discovery leans on search rather than scanning.

02Directory

The idea

Same unified surface, but as a four-up launcher grid of small tiles instead of a two-up card grid. A tile is a logo, a name, and one line of state. Connected tiles carry the accent ground so live things read at a glance; the catalog is the same object, unstyled.

Trade-off

Keeps browsing pleasant and scales to 40+ providers. Descriptions have to move to the detail page, so the tab tells you what you have more than what each thing does.

03Live rail + catalog shelf

The idea

Keeps a split, but redraws it along the line that actually matters: things that are running get rich cards with account, scopes and tool counts; everything else collapses into a compact shelf below. Nothing is labelled “one-click” — an unconnected provider is just a card you haven’t used yet.

Trade-off

The page transforms as you adopt it, which reads as progress. On a fresh install the rail is empty, so the first-run state needs its own treatment (shown at the top of the shelf).

04Browser

The idea

A left facet rail carries every cut of the data — status, category, operator — and the right pane is one uniform list that never changes shape. “Connected” becomes a filter, not a section, so the split disappears into a control the user drives.

Trade-off

The most scalable of the five and the most familiar (it is the shape of every app-store settings pane). It is also the most chrome for a catalog that is currently a dozen items — this design pays off at 50, not at 14.

05Control panel

The idea

Treats the tab as an operations surface, not a store. What is broken is called out at the top, what is running is a tight table with the facts an operator wants (account, tools, last used, host), and adding something is a single quick-add strip at the bottom — no catalog page, no second card style.

Trade-off

Best fit if integrations are mostly set-and-forget infrastructure. The catalog is deliberately demoted to a strip, so a growing provider list eventually needs an “All providers →” overlay.

06Picking one

If the catalog is the point

Option 2 (Directory). Keeps browsing and logos, kills the whitespace, and the connected/available split becomes a colour, not a heading.

If the connections are the point

Option 5 (Control panel). Answers “what do I have, what is broken, what can it do” in one screen; adding is a strip, not a page.

The safest middle

Option 1 (One list). Smallest departure from today’s data model — same cards’ information, one column, ranked by state. Ships without touching the API.

Shared fixes, whichever wins

Say “Coming soon” once, not twice. Give live connections the account name. Never reserve two lines of description for a tile that has none. Let status — not connector type — decide order.
